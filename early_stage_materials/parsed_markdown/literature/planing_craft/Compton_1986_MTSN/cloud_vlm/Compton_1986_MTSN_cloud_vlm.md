# Resistance of a Systematic Series of Semiplaning Transom-Stern Hulls

Roger H. Compton $^{1}$

The results of a systematic series of small (5 ft) models of hulls typical of coastal patrol, training, or recreational powerboats are presented and discussed. Hull form parameters studied include length-to-beam ratio, displacement-length ratio, longitudinal position of the center of gravity and section shape (hard chine or round bilge). The effects of these parameters on the calm-water resistance and running attitude (sinkage and trim) over a range of speeds corresponding to waterline length Froude numbers from 0.10 to 0.60 were investigated in the 120-ft towing tank at the U.S. Naval Academy Hydromechanics Laboratory (NAHL). Experimental procedures and computer-based data acquisition and analysis methods used at NAHL are described. The experimental results as well as the cross-faired and nondimensionalized stillwater resistance trends are presented. Comparisons with other resistance prediction methods for hulls of the subject type are made. An example of the application of the resistance prediction to the new 108-ft yard patrol craft (YP) being acquired by the U.S. Naval Academy is included.

The conceptual design of the new YP's began as a series of midshipman senior design projects in 1977 under the guidance of the author. Impetus for such a design effort was provided by the Naval Academy Superintendent (at the time) VADM Kinnard R. McKee, USN. Two of the midshipman design studies—one by (then) Midshipmen Corey Glab and Rich Maurer and the other by (then) Midshipmen Ron Miller and Mike Wertz of the Class of 1977—were the starting point for the design evolution at NAVSEA:NORFOLK which resulted in the 108-ft YP, the first of which, YP676, has completed her trial period, and is presently in service at the U.S. Naval Academy. YP676 and the initial six of her sister ships were (and are being) built by Peterson Builders, Inc. of Sturgeon Bay, Wisconsin. A photograph of YP676 underway serves as the frontispiece for this paper.

To avoid ambiguity as to the speed range of concern in this study, the term “semiplaning” requires definition. For the present purpose, a definition centering upon the sinkage of the hull’s center of gravity as a function of speed will be used. As a hull begins to move from rest, it generates a pressure field around it which causes vertical attitudinal changes (sinkage and trim). At zero and low speeds, the weight of the ship is

supported by buoyancy in accordance with Archimedes Principle. As speed increases and the pressure field deviates more and more from its original hydrostatic form, the effects of the resulting waves and pressure forces on the hull cause ever more significant translation (rise or sinkage) of the center of gravity and rotation (trim) about the same center. At very high speeds—planing speeds—the weight of the hull is supported primarily by high hydrodynamic pressures on the hull's bottom. These planing pressures can cause the hull to be lifted above its static absolute vertical position. If the absolute vertical position of the center of gravity is plotted as a function of hull speed, a curve like that shown in Fig. 1 is typically found. For the purposes of this paper, the three “speed regimes”—displacement, semiplaning, and planing—are defined in the figure. While arbitrary, the definitions as a function of what the center of gravity does are considered to be physically reasonable. Thus, the hull speeds covered in the results presented herein are within the “displacement” and “semiplaning” speed regimes.

The systematic series, whose stillwater resistance results are summarized here, was developed to assess the effects of practical hull shape and loading variations on the powering of such boats. This report covers but the first phase of testing on this series of hull forms. Seakeeping investigations concerning head sea motions, added resistance due to waves, and zero speed beam sea rolling are in progress or are planned. The present results are offered as the first installment in a set of tools for the small-craft designer to have in his professional toolkit.

# Description of the series test models

Six 5-ft ( $L_{\text{PP}}$ ) models were built to the lines shown in Fig. 2 by Alfred C. Seebode of Tabernacle-Vincentown, New Jersey. The models were constructed using 2-in. clear pine lifts faired to the molded shape given by the lines drawings. A nominal wall thickness of $1/2$ in. was maintained on the model topsides. To facilitate the mounting of towing apparatus, the upper surface of the bottom lift was parallel to, and 2 in. above, the base plane. Also, horizontal topped wedges were built into the sheer at the fore perpendicular and amidships to facilitate freeboard measurement by hook gage. The models were finished with multiple coats of clear spar varnish. Wet sanding after each coat (including the final coat) resulted in a smooth wetting surface. No external appendages were fitted to the series models.

The models were outfitted according to the normal procedures followed at the NAHL; that is, station lines were painted on the model with vertical scales at the ends and amidships to facilitate wave-hull intersection definition (and for subsequent testing in waves for relative motions), a vertical scale up the transom to quantify transom immersion, vertical threaded ballast rods fore and aft, a stud array (described later) for turbulence stimulation, and a mounting plate for connection to the towing dynamometry. Figure 6 is a photograph of one of the models ready for ballasting.

Because the experimental plan included testing each model at three different longitudinal positions of the models' center of gravity (LCG), and since NAHL's convention is to tow from the LCG, the dynamometry mounting plate locations had to be established. To minimize unwanted trimming moment due to the difference in the vertical position of the model towing point and the designed propeller shaft line, it was decided to position the towing point (physically, the trim pivot point on the towing dynamometer) on the hypothetical propeller shaft centerline at the longitudinal position of the model center of gravity. With the dynamometer trim pivot point thus located, the mounting plate location was completely defined. For all six hulls, the hypothetical propeller shaft centerline passed through a point 1.95 in. below the lowest point (on Q) of the model transom and made an angle of 9.75 deg with the design waterline. The dynamometry mounting plate was shimmed to maintain this condition for all LCG's tested.

Each model was to be tested at three LCG's for each of three displacements. The three LCG's, identical for all six models, were

Forward: 2.44% $L_{\mathrm{PP}}$ aft 20

Middle: 7.78% $L_{\mathrm{PP}}$ aft

Aft: 13.12% $L_{\mathrm{PP}}$ aft ②

These odd values correspond, nominally, to 45, 40, and 35 percent of $L_{\text{OA}}$ forward of the transom for YP81-1, and were specified by NAVSEA:NORFOLK. The three displacements correspond to zero-trim waterlines which were fixed distances below the reference waterline on each model. Specifically, for a 100-ft $L_{\text{PP}}$ , the drafts for testing were 0.15, 0.55, and 0.95 ft below the reference waterline.

1. For each model, the SHCP output (zero trim condition) was consulted to get displacement and wetted surface area at each test draft. The displacements were corrected for the towing tank water conditions; that is

4. When testing was imminent, the model, with the mounting plate fixed for a given LCG, was ballasted to a weight equal to (or slightly heavier than) the heaviest displacement. # Testing equipment and procedures

All resistance data were acquired in the 120-ft towing tank located in Rickover Hall at the U.S. Naval Academy. The International Towing Tank Conference (ITTC) description of this tank is shown as Fig. 7. The fresh-water depth for all tests was 5.5 ft. Blockage was not considered to be a problem with the test series since the blockage area ratio (maximum model sectional area divided by tank cross-sectional area) was less than 0.0045. Check tests of YP81-1 in a much larger towing tank (380 by 26 by 16 ft) verified that no measurable blockage effects were present in the smaller tank results.

The dynamometer used for all tests is shown in Fig. 8. The dynamometer restrained the model in surge, roll, sway, and yaw. The free motions of trim (pitch) and sinkage (heave) were measured by rotary potentiometers. Resistance force was measured by a 2-in. Hydronautics force block whose rated maximum load was 10 lb (block was linear to about ±100 percent of rated maximum load). The model speed was measured by an optical encoder activated by the carriage towing cable. All four transducers were calibrated (statically) before each test series and were rezeroed (electrically) between each run down the tank. All transducers demonstrated linear response over the anticipated ranges of the measured variables as can be seen from the sample force block calibration plot included as Fig. 9. The figure shows unretouched output from the laboratory's computer system. The resistance force is plotted on the ordinate, while the abscissa is directly proportional to a corresponding dc voltage (converted to a digital value). The coefficients for the least-squares linear fit (which are actually used in raw data conversion) are shown in the upper left corner of the calibration plot.

The force block remains horizontal regardless of model trim and thus measures pure resistance directly. The model was run from the wavemaker to the beach to minimize reflection of the model's transverse wave system. An interrun time of 10 min (nominal) was used for the entire stillwater resistance test series.

Data for each measured variable were acquired using the laboratory's computer-based data acquisition, control and analysis system [4]. Each channel of experimental data consisted of a system like the one shown schematically in Fig. 10. For the 120-ft tank, the "DATA TRANSMISSION SYSTEM" consists of a trailing cable from the carriage to the tank control station (the 380-ft tank employs a modulated laser as the link between the moving carriage and the shore-based data acquisition system). # Turbulence stimulation

The requirement that both model and ship experience similar flow regimes dictates that small models, such as those employed for this study, have turbulence stimulation devices fitted to trip the flow from a laminar to a turbulent regime. It is hoped that, once tripped, the flow remains turbulent over the length of the model hull. It is assumed that the added resistance caused by the stimulating device will be offset by the deficit in frictional resistance due to the laminar flow upstream of the device.

Studs were selected as the turbulence stimulation device for this series following standard NAHL practice. The final stud configuration, shown in Fig. 6, was chosen after considerable experimentation. There are three vertical rows of studs—at stations $1\frac{1}{2}$ , $2\frac{1}{2}$ , and $3\frac{1}{2}$ (of 10). The stud spacing in each row is $\frac{1}{2}$ in. Each stud is a right circular cylinder with a length (height) of $\frac{1}{10}$ in. and a diameter of $\frac{1}{8}$ in. The studs, made of brass, are attached to each hull with commercially available adhesive.

The stud configuration employed was chosen on the basis of a series of tests in which sand strips of various constant widths were placed near the bow of model YP81-1. Model YP81-1 was chosen for the stimulator study for both pragmatical and technical reasons. In the former category, it was the first hull model available for testing; in the latter category, it represents the middle of the hull form variations. # Model test results

A total of 54 separate model tests were conducted to acquire the data presented in this paper. For each of the six hulls, three displacements at each of three longitudinal centers of gravity were studied over a range of speeds corresponding to Froude numbers (based on waterline length) from 0.05 to 0.625. At each of 24 discrete model speeds $(v_{m})$ , model resistance $(R_{t_m})$ , model running trim angle $(\tau_{m})$ , and model sinkage $(Z_{\mathrm{CG}_m})$ were measured. The latter two variables were converted by the NAMB acquisition/analysis system to rise or sinkage at the fore and after perpendiculars (FP and AP). Figures 15 and 16 show typical raw data plots of total model resistance coefficient $(C_{t_m})$ versus Froude number and FP/AP rise versus Froude number, respectively. The results of three tests of a given hull at a given displacement are shown in each figure. Each plot symbol corresponds to a different LCG condition. Except at the extreme low-speed end of the speed range, the $C_{t_{m}}$ data are amazingly free of experimental scatter and make physical sense for the different LCG values.

Also plotted in Fig. 15 is the ITTC 1957 Ship-Model Correlation Line ( $C_{f_{m}}$ ), which was used for all resistance data reduction reported herein. The sinkage data (Fig. 16) tend to be more erratic than the resistance data. Similar data plots for all remaining tests were developed [5] for Models YP81-1 through YP81-6, respectively.

After the three sets of $C_{t_{m}}$ data were plotted, as in Fig. 15, smooth curves were faired through each set of data which represent the author's best estimate of the physical trends suggested by the data. Consistency among data for various LCG's (for a fixed displacement) and displacements (for a fixed LCG) were considered when fairing the family of $C_{t_{m}}$ curves for a given model.

The data shown in Figs. 15 and 16 represent bare hull conditions—no appendages of any sort were fitted. A limited series of tests on YP81-1 and YP81-4 was run in which centerline skegs were fitted [5].

# Series resistance results: numerical

The residual resistance coefficient data acquired from all tests were digitized and treated as the dependent variable values in a multiple linear regression analysis in which the hull shape and loading parameters of $L_{PP}/B$ , $\underline{\Delta}_{PP}$ , and $LCG/L_{PP}$ were treated as independent variables. Separate regression analyses were conducted at fixed speed parameter values. A series of BASIC programs was used to prepare the data for and to perform the multiple linear regression analysis [6]. The soft-chine and hard-chine hulls were run through the regression analysis routine separately.

$$

Notice the differences between the case study hull and those of which the presently reported standard series are composed. Most notably, the case study hull has a more generous bilge radius (to better suit planked wood construction), a full length integral skegkeel, and a less deeply immersed transom. The first and the last of these differences would suggest lower resistance at low to moderate speeds for the case study hull while the integral appendage would suggest a higher resistance at all speeds.

The standard powering estimators chosen for this comparison are as follows:

1. Taylor Standard Series [8]—Although the Taylor hull form is not at all similar to the present hull forms, this is a widely used benchmark for widely varying hull types. Inputs include prismatic coefficient $(C_p)$ , length-to-beam ratio $(L/B)$ , beam-to-draft ratio $(B/T)$ , and speed. At the high end of the speed range, the series is extrapolated beyond its original experimental data limits.   
2. Mercier and Savitsky's regression equation for transomsterned boats in the "preplaning" speed regime [9]—This estimate is based on many hull forms, hard- and soft-chined, operating at volumetric Froude numbers between 1.0 and 2.0. Inputs include angle of entrance at the static waterline $(i_{c})$ , transom sectional area to maximum sectional area ratio $(A_{7} / A_{\lambda})$ , a loading coefficient based on beam $(\nabla / B_{\lambda}^{3})$ , a volumetric coefficient $(L_{\mathrm{WL}} / \nabla^{1/3})$ , and a longitudinal center of gravity parameter (LCG/

$L_{PP}$ ). This estimator does not cover the low end of the speed range at all.

3. National Physical Laboratory (NPL) Series of Marwood and Bailey [2]—These hull forms are very similar in shape to the case study hull forms. Inputs include length-to-beam ratio $(L / B)$ and a volumetric coefficient $(\mathbf{M} = \mathrm{LWL} / \nabla^{1 / 3})$ . EHP values were read directly from curves drawn for a waterline length of 100 ft. A wetted surface was read from curves provided in the report.   
4. Series 63 of Beys [3]—A round bilged planing hull which was tested at low (subplaning) and high (planing) speeds. Inputs include length-to-beam ratio $(L / B)$ and a volumetric coefficient $(L_{\mathrm{TP}} / \nabla^{1 / 3})$ .   
5. Swedish Maritime Research Centre (SSPA) Series [10]-- The speed regime for this series is generally higher than that described here. Only the upper range of the case study hull's speed can be estimated by this reference. The plotted contours of $C_R$ for a block coefficient of 0.4 were increased by 5 percent on the basis of Appendix A of reference [10].

Neither Series 64 [1] nor the Webb Trawler series of Ridgely-Nevitt [11] was applicable to the present study because of displacement-length ratio mismatches. For all predictions the friction formulation employed by the series originator was used. All had the value of the correlation allowance, $C_A$ , set at 0.0004. The wetted surfaces used were those estimated by the predictors authors, not the actual wetted surface of the case study hull. This approach was adopted since each estimator normalized residual resistance (that is, calculated $C_R$ ) by using its own wetted surface and friction formulation.

The resulting effective horsepower estimates are shown in Fig. 36. Besides the five estimators listed above, the present series results—contours and four-term regression equation—were employed to estimate EHP. For the $C_R$ contours, linear interpolation was used over both $L_{CG}/L_{PP}$ and $L/B$ —and in that order. Similar linear interpolation was used to determine waterline length ( $L_{WL}$ ) and wetted surface from plotted S curves (see Fig. 4). The results are encouraging for the present series validity. The solid curve in Fig. 36 represents the EHP estimated from model testing a 5-ft geosim of the ship in a manner virtually identical to that used for the present series. Most of the estimators underpredict EHP, especially at low speeds. At high speeds, the severely extrapolated Taylor Series overpredicts badly, due to its cruiser stern and the attendant high trim by the stern. Neither Marwood and Bailey nor Mercier and Savitsky is applicable at the low end of the speed range. Series 63 gives the best estimate for the upper speed regime (above 15 knots for $L_{PP} = 101.7$ ft). It overpredicts EHP throughout the speed range, unlike most of the other estimators attempted.

The contours and regression equation developed in this report overpredict EHP at low speeds (up to 13 knots) and under-predict at high speeds (over 16 knots) for the case study presented. However, the present series is reasonably close over the entire speed range. How well each predictor does at estimating EHP is shown quantitatively in Table 3. Several more case study situations should be studied before any widespread acceptance of the present series work is established.

The following trends were observed:

1. The aftmost center-of-gravity loading condition results in the highest total and residual resistance coefficients (and running trim angle) for all hulls.   
2. The heaviest displacement has the highest total and residual resistance coefficients for all hulls.   
3. The difference in resistance due to LCG differences lessens or disappears at the highest speeds tested.   
4. The primary hump in the residual resistance coefficient curves occurs near a volumetric Froude number of 1.2.   
5. Hard-chine hulls have slightly lower residual resistance coefficients than soft-chine hulls of the same nominal length-to-beam ratio and loading ( $\alpha_{PP}$ and LCG/L) at a given speed. The difference generally becomes more pronounced at higher speeds.   
6. More slender hulls within the series have higher residual resistance coefficients than the beamier hulls.

Trends 5 and 6 may appear contrary to intuition for designers. It must be remembered that the trends stated apply to residual resistance coefficients only. Differences in wetted surface caused by differing hull slenderness may result in higher resistance forces and horsepowers.
